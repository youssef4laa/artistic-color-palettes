# Wada's method: how the book says to use its combinations

Source: Sanzo Wada, *配色総鑑* (1933–34), as reprinted in *配色事典* (Seigensha, 2010). Everything
below is our paraphrase. Citations give the printed page, then the PDF page of the scan
(`p. 20 / PDF 22`); in the front matter PDF = printed + 2. The reprint keeps only the second half
of Wada's booklet *概説と其活用* (the part on combinations), in modern kanji (p. 4 / PDF 6;
p. 12 / PDF 14). Lines starting "How the skill uses this" are our design decisions, not Wada's.

## 1. What the book is (2010 editor's framing, Ōmi Gentarō, pp. 3–14 / PDF 5–16)

**1.1 Structure.** Six volumes, 348 combinations: 120 two-colour, 120 three-colour, 108
four-colour, published 1933–34 (p. 12 / PDF 14). In the reprint plates 1–120 are pairs, 121–240
triads, 241–348 quads (contents p. 5 / PDF 7; first quad, plate 241, p. 150 / PDF 152).
How the skill uses this: plate numbers outside 1–348 are invalid; plate size (2/3/4) is a filter.

**1.2 The reprint is a reproduction.** The originals were hand-painted matte papers, larger than
the reprint; the reprint lists CMYK values in its index (p. 12 / PDF 14; p. 4 / PDF 6).
How the skill uses this: every hex/OKLCH value is an approximation of a print of a hand-painted
chip; the skill says so and never claims "Wada's exact colour".

**1.3 Names are period names.** Colour names follow the original and carry their era's meaning,
so they may not match what a modern reader pictures for that name (p. 4 / PDF 6).
How the skill uses this: names are labels only; decisions use the measured values.

**1.4 Principle versus personal taste.** Wada accepts that liking a colour is subjective but still
aims to guide good and bad choices. The editor reads him as saying that rule-made combinations
are unoriginal and ordinary, yet still worth having for everyday use (p. 13 / PDF 15).
How the skill uses this: plates are offered as vetted starting points; the user's taste picks
among them, and the skill never calls a palette "harmonious" merely because a rule says so.

**1.5 Two viewpoints, ten patterns.** The editor summarises Wada's composition theory as two
viewpoints split into ten patterns, rooted in long-standing European practice (p. 13 / PDF 15).
These are the same-tone and different-tone families of five each (section 3).
How the skill uses this: the ten patterns are the vocabulary for describing a plate's character.

**1.6 Four-colour plates have internal pairs.** In a quad, the top and bottom colours relate to
each other, the middle-left and middle-right relate to each other, and the four together make one
statement (p. 13 / PDF 15). The quad plates use a cross layout (e.g. p. 150 / PDF 152).
How the skill uses this: when mapping a quad to roles, keep each pair together (for example one
pair for ground and surface, the other for primary and accent) rather than splitting them.

**1.7 Let the colours speak.** The editor stresses that the book shows rather than lectures: it
asks the reader to study the swatches themselves (p. 13 / PDF 15).
How the skill uses this: always show swatches before any explanation or theory.

**1.8 No order-system harmony theory.** Unlike Munsell or Ostwald, Wada did not derive harmony
from a colour-order system; the plates come from a painter's experience (pp. 13–14 / PDF 15–16).
Wada's colour research had three strands — naming colours, ordering them, and combining
them — and the third rests on the first two (p. 9 / PDF 11).
How the skill uses this: OKLCH is for building ramps and measuring contrast, not for judging
whether a Wada pairing "works"; the pairing's authority is the plate.

## 2. Wada's own prefaces (pp. 16–18 / PDF 18–20)

**2.1 Words cannot carry a combination.** Wada argues that recording a combination with abstract
names (blue, red, cream, rose and so on) conveys nothing to a non-specialist and cannot pin down
the exact hue, which is why the book uses physical samples (p. 17 / PDF 19).
How the skill uses this: every palette it hands over carries values and a swatch preview, never
names alone.

**2.2 A consultant, possibly a guide.** He intends the book as an easy everyday colour advisor
that may also serve as a somewhat decisive guide (pp. 17–18 / PDF 19–20). The foreword by Wada
Eisaku says everyone combines colours, but finding subtle harmonies takes a trained, experienced
eye (p. 16 / PDF 18).
How the skill uses this: prefer an existing plate over a freshly generated harmony, and present
recommendations as advice the user can overrule.

## 3. Composition: 配色の構成 (pp. 18–22 / PDF 20–24)

**3.1 Two sides of composition.** Composition can be traditional or habitual — low in originality
but well fitted to real life, i.e. ordinary — or individually invented: novel, sometimes seen as
eccentric, artistically interesting even when it misses its purpose (p. 18 / PDF 20).
How the skill uses this: offer a conventional mode (safe, familiar role mapping) and an
expressive mode (unexpected role mapping), and say which one a suggestion belongs to.

**3.2 A canon is a pillar, not a mould.** A rule for combinations risks forcing work into a
template, but nothing is built without some foundation; a standard keeps you from going astray and
is not a demand to copy (p. 19 / PDF 21).
How the skill uses this: proportion targets and pattern labels are defaults; departures are fine
when the user states a reason.

**3.3 Nature is the root.** Harmony ultimately follows nature (light), not human invention, and
nature shows that harmony can arise even among entirely unlike tones (p. 19 / PDF 21).
How the skill uses this: different-hue palettes are legitimate, not a failure of restraint.

**3.4 Two kinds of harmony.** Harmony need not come from raising or lowering one colour's
strength; as in music, Wada calls gradual modulation of tone *同色調* (same tone) and the attempt
to harmonise opposed tones *異色調* (different tone). He reads colour by hue, 色度 and lightness
(pp. 19–20 / PDF 21–22). [uncertain: 色度 is a prewar term; we read it as chroma/saturation]
How the skill uses this: every plate is tagged same-tone or different-tone from its OKLCH hues.

**3.5 The ten tone patterns** (p. 20 / PDF 22; restated in our words; "UI meaning" is ours).
Wada describes only the same-tone sub-types one by one; for the different-tone ones he says only
that they seek harmony from wholly different colour qualities, so the different-tone readings
below apply the same five modifiers by analogy. [uncertain: different-tone sub-types inferred]

| Wada term | Our reading | UI meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 明同色調 bright same-tone | related hues, all light, equal strength | airy one-family light theme; text needs a dark neutral from outside the set |
| 暗同色調 dark same-tone | related hues, all dark, similar character | one-family dark theme; text needs a light neutral |
| 淡同色調 pale same-tone | neighbouring hues, same tone, strength lowered | muted low-chroma theme for calm tools and docs; accent must come from a chroma step |
| 濃又深同色調 deep same-tone | strong, dense, deep related colours (Wada cites folk textiles and buildings) | saturated brand-heavy sections; poor for long reading surfaces |
| 汎同色調 pan same-tone | related hues mixing light, dark, pale and deep freely ("pan" from Greek) | the classic single-hue ramp (50–950) of a design system |
| 明異色調 bright different-tone | unlike hues, all light | light multi-hue sets: tags, category fills on light ground |
| 暗異色調 dark different-tone | unlike hues, all dark | dark multi-hue sets for dark mode surfaces |
| 淡異色調 pale different-tone | unlike hues, all muted | muted categorical palettes, quiet charts |
| 濃又深異色調 deep different-tone | unlike hues, all strong and deep | bold posters, expressive landing pages |
| 汎異色調 pan different-tone | unlike hues across all lightness and strength | full palette with distinct roles; hardest to control |

How the skill uses this: classify a chosen plate into one row, then pick the matching strategy —
same-tone rows for restrained palettes, different-tone rows for full-palette work.

**3.6 The table is a guide, not a law.** One may merge the two families or break them to make a
new tone; forcing nature into categories is rigid. But fusing or breaking them well needs long
experience with colour quantity and colour quality (p. 21 / PDF 23).
How the skill uses this: mixed or rule-breaking palettes get stricter checks (proportion preview,
contrast on every pair) before they are recommended.

**3.7 "Same" means same family.** Wada's kimono example runs from a sky-blue outer coat through an
ultramarine-tinged robe and greenish sash to a yellowish-green collar: same-tone means a family of
related colours, not one hue (p. 21 / PDF 23).
How the skill uses this: a restrained palette may drift to neighbouring hues; it does not have to
be strictly monochrome. (Any hue tolerance number the skill uses is our own choice.)

**3.8 Safe versus interesting.** For dress, interiors and furniture, the pan same-tone approach is
safe and fairly easy for anyone; different-tone combination is truly difficult but offers more
interest (pp. 21–22 / PDF 23–24).
How the skill uses this: default to the restrained (same-tone) strategy; offer the full-palette
(different-tone) strategy when the user wants character and accepts extra checking.

## 4. Colour quality, material and ground: 色質、色材と生地との関係 (pp. 22–23 / PDF 24–25)

**4.1 Colour quality.** Colours differ in quality: transparent or opaque, warm or cool. Warmth
varies inside a hue family — some greens read warm and others cool, and a canary yellow is cooler
than turmeric or kerria yellows (p. 22 / PDF 24).
How the skill uses this: warm/cool is computed per colour from its value, not assumed from its
hue name, when balancing temperature across roles.

**4.2 Material changes the colour.** The same colour becomes oil paint, watercolour or dye, and
can turn transparent or opaque by material: thinned with water it goes transparent, thinned with
white it goes opaque (p. 22 / PDF 24).
How the skill uses this: a tint made by alpha over the background and a tint mixed toward white
are different colours; tokens use solid mixed values (measurable contrast), and alpha is kept for
overlays and states.

**4.3 The ground changes the colour.** The same colour and material dyed on silk, cotton or hemp
gives clearly different results; different materials on different grounds differ even more, and
a sensitive eye always notices (pp. 22–23 / PDF 24–25).
How the skill uses this: the page background is the ground; every palette is judged on its real
background, and a dark mode is a new ground, so it is recomposed rather than inverted.

## 5. Quantity of colour: 彩色の量と配色 (pp. 23–24 / PDF 25–26)

**5.1 Quantity means area.** Quantity is the amount, i.e. the area, of each applied colour. The
book shows every swatch at equal area only for convenience (p. 23 / PDF 25).
How the skill uses this: a plate's equal swatches say nothing about proportions; the skill
always adds proportions.

**5.2 Avoid equal amounts.** In real dress or design, using colours in equal amount and identical
shape is rare and should usually be avoided (p. 23 / PDF 25).
How the skill uses this: never assign equal area to palette roles; each palette gets explicit
proportion targets (the exact percentages are our convention, not Wada's).

**5.3 A clear hierarchy of area.** In a woman's kimono, the collar shows a tiny amount, the robe
and outer coat take most of the area, and the sash comes next (p. 23 / PDF 25).
How the skill uses this: roles follow a dominant / secondary / accent ladder; for a triad, the
skill decides which plate colour plays the tiny "collar" accent.

**5.4 Test proportions with cut-out windows.** Wada supplied cut cards (a triangle, a rectangle and
a wave) to lay over the plates and vary shape and quantity, calling this the most direct route to
a good combination; for more colours, make your own windows (pp. 23–24 / PDF 25–26). With the
triangle on a three-colour plate, the narrow tip gives the collar, then robe, then sash or coat.
[uncertain: the exact placement of the triangle over the three chips is not fully explicit]
How the skill uses this: before handing over, the skill renders a proportion preview (a mock
layout at target areas) so the user judges the palette at its real ratios.

**5.5 Large areas look weaker.** A colour spread over a large area looks much paler and weaker
than the small chip in the book; keep this in mind whenever applying the plates (pp. 23–24 /
PDF 25–26). (Some modern studies report large areas looking lighter and more saturated; either
way, a chip does not predict a wall.)
How the skill uses this: background and large-surface colours are previewed at full size, never
chosen from a small swatch alone.

**5.6 The white mount is a colour.** Treat the white album paper the chips sit on as a white
swatch, and study its combinations with each chip using the cut cards too (p. 24 / PDF 26).
How the skill uses this: the page background (white or not) counts as a palette member, with its
own area share and its own contrast checks against every role.
